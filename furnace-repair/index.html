A modern furnace runs a strict startup sequence, and knowing where the sequence breaks is most of the diagnosis. The thermostat calls, the inducer motor spins up to prove draft through a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter glows, the gas valve opens, the burners light, the flame sensor confirms fire, and only then does the blower deliver heat to the house. In Wenatchee, WA, the failures cluster at predictable points: an oxide-coated flame sensor, a hot surface igniter cracked from years of thermal cycling, a pressure switch that will not close because the inducer is weak or the condensate trap on a high-efficiency model has clogged, or a control board with a burned relay. Each one stops the sequence cold, and each one mimics the others from the hallway.

Wenatchee HVAC repairs furnaces by testing the sequence, not guessing at it. Our experienced technicians verify each stage with a meter rather than a hunch: igniter resistance, flame signal in microamps, pressure switch operation against actual inducer draft, gas valve voltage, and blower and capacitor health. Every visit includes the safety fundamentals on a fuel-burning appliance: heat exchanger inspection, leak-testing of gas connections, and flue draft verification, because the difference between a furnace problem and a furnace hazard is not visible from the thermostat. We measure temperature rise against the data plate before we leave so the fire and the airflow are matched the way the manufacturer intended.

We work on natural gas, propane, and electric furnaces from every major brand, both conventional 80 percent units and condensing high-efficiency models with their traps, drains, and dual pressure switches. The truck stock covers the parts that actually fail: igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors, so most no-heat calls end with heat the same visit. Warning Signs for Furnace Repair

Spotting the early warning signs gives you the chance to act before the situation spirals into something far more expensive. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Burners light, run a few seconds, then shut down repeatedly
  • A bang or boom when the burners ignite late
  • Squealing or grinding from the blower compartment
  • Rust streaks, soot, or water around the cabinet or flue
  • The furnace clicks and glows but the burners never light
  • Yellow, wavering burner flames where they should be sharp blue
  • Frequent on-off cycling that never reaches the thermostat setting
  • The blower runs constantly but the air never warms

Why Professional Furnace Repair Matters

The most serious furnace problem is usually hidden. A cracked or corroded heat exchanger can let combustion gases into the airstream, and nothing about it is visible from the outside of the cabinet. Delayed ignition makes things worse, because each boom at light-off puts more stress on that metal. Add high voltage, a gas valve, and a flue to the same box, and the margin for error shrinks. Diagnosis is full of traps too. A high limit that keeps tripping could be a bad switch, or it could be doing exactly its job because a filter is clogged or the blower is dying, and swapping out a working switch only hides the real fault.

Our experienced technicians treat every symptom as a question about the whole system. Flame signal gets measured rather than assumed, so a healthy sensor is never replaced while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve skates by. Draft is verified at the pressure switch and the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, which is how airflow problems get caught before they shorten the exchanger's life. And the gas side is leak-tested with instruments, not a sniff and a shrug. The result is a furnace that has been proven safe and correct under a full cycle, and an honest report if the unit is nearing the point where replacement beats another repair.

How to Prevent Furnace Repair Problems

Smart maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • If you have a high-efficiency furnace, check that the condensate drain is flowing freely
  • Store laundry supplies, especially chlorine products, well away from the furnace closet
  • Replace the filter every month while the furnace is working hard, since it costs less than any other part
  • Put a carbon monoxide detector on every level and test them monthly
  • Make sure the furnace has clear combustion air; a packed utility room starves the burners
  • Schedule service each fall to catch worn igniters and dirty sensors before cold weather arrives
  • Pay attention when the burners light, since a boom instead of a soft whoosh calls for service
